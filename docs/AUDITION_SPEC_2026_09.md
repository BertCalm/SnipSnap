# AUDITION — choosing a sound in the context it will live in

*A design spec, September 2026. Comes out of a conversation rather than a
code reading, so the closing section marks carefully which parts the owner
decided, which are proposed here and unratified, and which are not verified
at all. Every question this spec opened has since been answered — see
**Decisions**.*

---

## Why this exists

The app is two things: an instrument builder and a beat maker. Both matter.
But they are not used the same way, and the difference turns out to be the
whole design.

Asked which he starts from, the owner's answer was **both, about equally
often — but more engaged and invested in the sound design.** Equal
frequency, unequal investment. That asymmetry is the hinge:

- **Beat mode wants momentum.** Don't ask questions, don't make me name
  things, give me something playable in seconds. Interruption is the enemy.
- **Sound design mode wants depth.** Let me hear the difference, hold two
  versions side by side, go back, sit with it. Time is not the enemy —
  losing my place is.

"Do both well" therefore does *not* mean treat them the same. Today the app
offers one shape to both: a menu of verbs, one flow, one current state.

**There is corroborating evidence that the invested mode was under-served.**
Every one of the three data-loss defects fixed in the September repair wave
lived in the sound-design half — `MAKE PAD ▸` overwriting a render it could
never reproduce, DRIFT blending at 0% while drawing 50%, EXPORT replacing a
kit behind an invisible arm. Not a coincidence: those surfaces were built as
*tools*, and a tool does not need undo, compare, or versions. A workspace
you inhabit does. The repair wave fixed the three worst instances; this spec
is about the shape underneath them.

## The observation the whole thing rests on

**The app has already listened, and spends what it learns on labels rather
than offers.**

By the time a player is staring at a menu, the app has classified the
material (`Classifier`), found the hits, measured the tempo, guessed the
key, and — through `Balance` — has an actual opinion about where a sound of
that class should sit against a kick. It knows more about the material than
the player does. It uses that to *annotate*, never to *propose*.

AUDITION is the first place it proposes.

---

## What it is

> You have two versions of a sound and can't choose. AUDITION plays them
> **inside the pattern you are already working in**, swapping on the bar
> line, level-matched, without telling you which is which. You listen until
> you know. Then you reveal, and keep listening — now labelled — to
> understand *what the difference was*.

Later, a third candidate generated by the app joins the rotation and has to
explain itself in one line. That part is **v2** — v1 is the player's own two
takes (decision 4), which is already the whole of the problem that prompted
this.

---

## The five rules, and why each one is load-bearing

### 1. Keep every candidate — including after the choice

Nothing is overwritten to make room for a comparison, and nothing is
destroyed by the outcome of one. This is the rule the repair wave arrived at
from the other direction: `MAKE PAD ▸` now leaves `DRONE`, `DRONE 2`,
`DRONE 3` side by side instead of destroying each in turn. That was a bug
fix, but it is also the first time this app let someone hold several
versions of a sound and pick — the seed AUDITION grows from.

Because those siblings already exist as named things on the shelf,
"choosing" is only *assignment*: the winner goes into the pad slot and the
others stay exactly where they were, named and playable. See decision 1.

### 2. Play it in the pattern, in the mix — not solo

Solo-against-the-kit is a *masking* test: does this fight the kick for
space. Quick, repeatable, and the app can measure alongside the ears.

In-the-pattern is a *feel* test: does it land right, does it groove, does it
vanish when everything is playing. Less tractable, much truer to how the
sound gets used.

**Chosen: in the pattern.** It is the harder build and the real question.

### 3. Alternate on the bar — never a button

This is what makes rule 2 survivable.

A/B discrimination collapses as the gap between the two grows. Under about a
second, fine differences are audible; at eight bars apart the player is
comparing *memories* and will mostly prefer whichever was heard last. That
is normally the fatal objection to in-context comparison.

Alternating automatically on the bar line closes the gap — at 90–120bpm the
same candidate returns roughly every two seconds — while keeping everything
that made in-context the right choice. It also means the player operates
nothing, and so has nothing to think about except the sound.

### 4. Level-match, or the test does not merely fail — it misleads

In any blind comparison the louder candidate wins, near-universally, and
listeners cannot hear past it. Two versions of a snare a decibel apart will
produce a confident, repeatable, *wrong* answer.

This is not a new capability. `Loudness.of` measures perceived level in
`:audio`, and `Punch.rescaleToLoudness` already rescales a buffer to a
loudness target. `Punch`'s own KDoc names the reason in the app's words:
rescaling exists so that PUNCH is not judged on "the loudness-war confound a
transient/saturation stage should not be". AUDITION faces exactly that
confound and inherits the answer.

*Skipping this is worse than not building the feature.*

### 5. Blind first; reveal when ready; keep looping

Asked blind or labelled, the answer was **both** — and there is an order
that makes that a sequence rather than a compromise.

- **Blind** protects the first impression, the only uncontaminated data the
  player gets. It matters most for the app's own candidate: labelled, it is
  rejected on principle or accepted out of novelty, and neither is a
  judgement about the sound.
- **Labelled** answers a different and equally useful question — not "which
  is better" but "what did it *do*, and do I want that move?"

The order is a **one-way door**: blind → labelled is possible, the reverse
is not. So the reveal is a deliberate act the player takes, never a timer
that fires after four bars and quietly spends the one honest listen. **The
loop does not stop at the reveal.**

If three candidates in rotation is too long a cycle to hold in the ear, run
it as a knockout — A against B, winner against C — so every comparison stays
two bars.

## The third candidate

The app's own version of the sound, in the rotation, unlabelled until the
reveal.

**It must explain itself in one line.** *"Pulled 200–400Hz to make room for
your kick."* *"Matched the room on your hats."* A black box that says
*this one is better* is exactly what someone invested in their own sound
design will distrust and stop using — correctly. It is also the rule this
codebase already lives by: never claim, say what happened.

**If it cannot explain itself in a sentence, it should not be offered.**

There is a second reason this matters. The explanation is how the app
*teaches*. Heard labelled, looping, with a one-line account of the move, the
player learns the move and does it themselves next kit. That turns the
suggestion from a vending machine into a second pair of ears — which is the
right relationship for someone who is invested in the craft rather than
wanting it done for them.

## What already exists

Verified by reading, not assumed. This feature is mostly assembly.

| Piece | Where | What it gives AUDITION |
|---|---|---|
| `Loudness.of` | `:audio` | Perceived level of a candidate |
| `Punch.rescaleToLoudness` | `:synth` | Rule 4, already written and already justified |
| `Balance` + `Balance.TARGETS` | `:kit` | Where a class *should* sit relative to the kick — "the difference between sounds like a kit and sounds like files" |
| `Classifier.classify` | `:audio` | What the candidate is, so `TARGETS` applies |
| `GrooveProgram`, FEEL | `:kit`, `:shell` | The pattern to audition inside |
| Kit playback engine | `:app` native | Playing it |
| Fresh-name-per-press | `SnipStore`/`OneNote` | Rule 1 — siblings instead of replacement |

**What is genuinely new:** the bar-accurate candidate swap while the loop
runs, the audition's own state (candidates, blind/revealed, knockout
bracket), the generator behind the third candidate, and the screen.

The swap is the real engineering. Everything else is wiring.

## Decisions

Six questions were left open by the first draft of this spec. All six were
put to the owner with options and a recommendation, and all six
recommendations were accepted. Recorded with their reasons, because the
reasons are what a later reader will need in order to reopen one honestly.

### 1. Nothing happens to the losing candidates

*Considered:* bin them; keep them as alternates attached to the pad; keep
them silently until export.

Alternates-on-a-pad is a kit-format change that would dwarf the feature.
Silent keeping is invisible accumulating state — the exact category of
defect the September repair wave existed to remove. Both were rejected.

**The question largely dissolves**, because the candidates are already
separate named siblings on the shelf. Choosing assigns the winner to the pad
slot; the others are neither destroyed nor hidden. No bin trip, no format
change, and no new work — it fell out of the `MAKE PAD ▸` fix.

**This settles the largest fork in the spec: AUDITION is a *comparison*
feature, not a versions feature.**

### 2. Entered from the pad sheet, borrowing the kit for context

*Considered:* pad sheet; kit screen; both.

The kit screen is where the context lives, which makes it the tempting
answer. But the moment of indecision happens on the **pad sheet**, seconds
after the second version was made. That screen is already the densest in the
app — and one more button there still beats a navigation trip away from the
work.

One entry point in v1. A kit-screen route can follow if it turns out to be
wanted; the app already has precedent for several routes into one place.

### 3. Always two candidates at a time

*Considered:* two; three; unbounded with a knockout.

Two bars is roughly two seconds at working tempos — inside the window where
real differences are audible. Three bars is about six seconds, which puts
the player back to comparing memories, the precise failure the whole design
exists to avoid.

More than two is handled by **structure** — a knockout bracket — never by a
longer rotation. A useful side effect: v1 and v3 then share one core loop
instead of needing two.

### 4. The app's third candidate is not in v1

*Considered:* v1; later.

It is the differentiating idea, which is exactly why it should not be rushed
— it needs a generator, an explanation, and taste, and it is the part most
likely to ship half-convincing and poison the feature.

Blind in-pattern A/B between the player's own two takes is already the
entire value for the problem that prompted this, and it proves the hard part
(the bar-accurate swap). There will also be far better evidence about what a
*good* third candidate is after living with v1 on real material than can be
specified now.

### 5. Level and EQ; PUNCH a maybe; never pitch or time

*Considered:* level only; level + EQ; + transient shaping; + pitch/time.

The one-line-explanation rule is the filter, and it turns out to be a sharp
one. *"Brought it down 2dB to sit under your kick"* and *"pulled 200–400Hz"*
both explain themselves and both answer the masking question that kit
context is actually about. *"Sharpened the attack"* explains itself too, and
`Punch` already exists.

*"Re-pitched it"* does not qualify. A re-pitched or time-stretched candidate
is a **different sound**, not a better-fitting one, and it breaks the
comparison it was entered into. Fit is the question; identity is not.

### 6. Not gated by material

*Considered:* drums only; everything, with degraded help.

Level-matching and bar-alternation are entirely material-agnostic, and they
are the bulk of the value. Only the third candidate's *advice* needs a class
to reason from, and `Balance.TARGETS` has entries for `TONAL`, `LOOP` and
`UNKNOWN` — less to say about them, but not nothing.

Since v1 has no generator, the question does not become real until v2, by
which point there will be evidence. Do not gate it.

### What the six have in common

Five of the six make v1 **smaller**. That is not caution. The hard, novel,
valuable thing here is the blind swap-on-the-bar comparison; every other
decision turned out to be free (1), deferrable (4, 5, 6), or a constraint
rather than a feature (3).

---

## Phasing

- **v1** — two candidates, in the player's own pattern, alternating on the
  bar, loudness-matched, blind with a manual reveal, entered from the pad
  sheet. No generator. Any material. Losers stay as named siblings.
- **v2** — the app's third candidate, level and EQ only, with its one-line
  explanation; and whatever question 5's PUNCH maybe becomes.
- **v3** — knockout brackets for more than two candidates.

The v1 build is essentially one piece of real engineering — a
sample-accurate candidate swap at the bar line while the loop runs — plus
the audition's own state and a screen. Everything else in the table above is
wiring.

## Provenance

**Decided by the owner.** In a pattern rather than solo; keep both until
decided; testing against the sounds already chosen in the kit matters; a
third app-optimised option is wanted; blind *and* labelled. Then, on the six
questions above: all six recommendations accepted as written.

**Proposed here and not separately ratified:** alternating on the bar rather
than a manual switch; the blind → reveal ordering and its one-way-door
argument; loudness-matching as a hard rule; the one-line-explanation
requirement.

**Not verified anywhere:** any of it on a phone. `:app` has no Kotlin test
source set, so nothing here has been heard. The claims about existing
building blocks were checked by reading the source; the claims about what
will *sound* right were not, and cannot be from here.
